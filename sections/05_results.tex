\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Structured Residuals Left by the Aligned Prior}
\label{subsec:residual_structure_results}

The first question is whether the affine-aligned DeLaurier-style prior leaves a residual that is worth correcting with data. Figures~\ref{fig:phase_residual_measured}--\ref{fig:frequency_residual_measured} show three diagnostic views used in this paper: wingbeat-phase medians, condition-binned RMSE, and frequency-band energy. In each view, the aligned-prior residual is compared with the fitted correction and the remaining residual after correction. This order makes the correction a response to observed simulator-to-flight structure rather than an unrestricted replacement of the prior.

  \caption{Wingbeat-phase-binned residual medians for the measured-mass-properties branch. The phase-structured correction follows the repeatable phase-dependent force residuals left by the aligned prior and reduces the remaining phase waveform.}
  \label{fig:phase_residual_measured}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[t]

  \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/condition_residual_rmse_measured_massprops.pdf}
  \caption{Condition-binned residual RMSE for key variables. The correction reduces the aligned-prior residual across the retained flight envelope, while the lateral force and some moment channels remain less uniform across conditions.}
  \label{fig:condition_residual_measured}
\end{figure*}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{figures/frequency_residual_energy_measured_massprops.pdf}
  \caption{Demeaned residual energy by frequency band. The axial and vertical force residuals retain strong wingbeat-frequency structure before correction, and the correction removes most of that dominant band energy on the held-out logs.}
  \label{fig:frequency_residual_measured}
\end{figure*}

\textit{Phase structure.} The axial and vertical force residuals contain repeatable wingbeat-phase waveforms. For $f_{x,b}$, phase-bin medians explain $0.860$ of the aligned-prior residual variance and the true residual has a peak-to-peak phase amplitude of $13.46~\mathrm{N}$. For $f_{z,b}$, the phase $R^2$ is $0.906$ and the peak-to-peak amplitude is $25.40~\mathrm{N}$. After correction, the remaining phase peak-to-peak amplitudes fall to $3.07~\mathrm{N}$ and $4.76~\mathrm{N}$, respectively. The lateral force channel has weaker phase structure, with phase $R^2=0.156$, which is consistent with its weaker prediction score in Table~\ref{tab:measured_massprops_target_results}.

\textit{Flight-condition dependence.} The residual also varies across operating conditions. Across cycle-flapping-frequency bins, aligned-prior residual RMSE ranges from $3.58$ to $5.46~\mathrm{N}$ for $f_{x,b}$ and from $6.72$ to $10.58~\mathrm{N}$ for $f_{z,b}$. The highest-frequency bin is the worst bin for both channels in the current test split. After correction, the worst-bin errors become $1.93~\mathrm{N}$ and $3.22~\mathrm{N}$. This shows that the correction reduces structured mismatch inside the retained flight envelope while still leaving condition-dependent error in the more difficult bins.

\textit{Frequency-domain structure.} After subtracting segment means, the dominant residual energy in $f_{x,b}$ is at the wingbeat fundamental, which carries $51.6\%$ of the residual energy. For $f_{z,b}$, the wingbeat fundamental carries $78.7\%$ of the residual energy. The remaining energy in these dominant bands is $2.51\%$ and $3.12\%$ of the original band energy, respectively. This frequency-localized reduction supports the interpretation that the correction removes repeatable wingbeat-synchronous discrepancy left by the low-dimensional prior.

These analyses do not prove that the residual is a single aerodynamic mechanism. The residual may combine wing flexibility, unsteady loading, actuator timing, tail/body coupling, outdoor disturbance, airdata error, and label reconstruction effects. The useful engineering conclusion is narrower: real-flight data reveal structured simulator-to-flight effective-wrench discrepancy, and a constrained correction map can reduce that discrepancy on held-out logs.

\subsection{Channel Excitation and Correctability}
\label{subsec:channel_correctability}

The second question is why the correction is stronger in some channels than others. Table~\ref{tab:channel_correctability} summarizes channel excitation and correctability diagnostics on the held-out split. The channels with the strongest final prediction, $f_{x,b}$, $f_{z,b}$, and $m_{y,b}$, also have lower normalized error and stronger explanatory structure from deployable state, actuation, airdata, and phase features. The weaker channels have higher normalized error and more broadband residual energy.

The force-channel pattern is consistent with the residual-structure figures. The axial and vertical force channels have sustained excitation and repeatable wingbeat-frequency residual content, so a phase- and condition-dependent correction can remove a large part of the aligned-prior mismatch. The lateral force channel remains harder: its normalized error is close to the target variation scale, and its residual is more sensitive to sideslip, wind disturbance, and lateral excitation.

The moment-channel pattern is more cautious. The $m_{y,b}$ channel is the most predictable moment output in the current split, but $m_{x,b}$ and $m_{z,b}$ remain lower-SNR effective rotational-wrench channels. A roll-specific diagnostic gives the same conclusion: restricting $m_{x,b}$ to high-roll-transient subsets does not make the channel reliable. The $m_{x,b}$ normalized error is $0.881$ on all test samples, $0.848$ for $|\dot{p}|$ above the $75$th percentile, and $0.884$ for $|\dot{p}|$ above the $90$th percentile. This suggests that the roll-moment limitation is not weak excitation alone; it is also consistent with angular-derivative, reference-point, timing, and smoothing sensitivity.

\subsection{Structured Correction Performance}
\label{subsec:prediction_performance}

Given the residual structure above, the correction is evaluated as a log-conditioned effective-wrench predictor. Table~\ref{tab:correction_model_summary} summarizes the measured-mass-properties rerun on the fixed held-out test split. The affine-aligned DeLaurier-style prior gives a mean force-channel RMSE of $4.738~\mathrm{N}$ across $f_{x,b}$, $f_{y,b}$, and $f_{z,b}$. Adding a structured force correction reduces this mean force RMSE to $1.806~\mathrm{N}$. The deployable correction, which uses only simulator-available state, airdata, phase, frequency, body-rate, and control features, further reduces mean force RMSE to $1.644~\mathrm{N}$. The final phase-structured correction keeps this validation-selected force branch and changes mainly the effective-moment head and residual-diagnostic basis. Its mean force RMSE therefore remains $1.644~\mathrm{N}$, while mean moment RMSE decreases from $0.383~\mathrm{N\,m}$ to $0.365~\mathrm{N\,m}$ relative to the deployable correction. Thus the force-channel gain should be attributed to the deployable structured force correction, whereas the final row is used to report the channel-aware effective-moment fit and the phase-residual diagnostics consistently.

The per-channel results in Table~\ref{tab:measured_massprops_target_results} show where the correction is strongest. Relative to the affine-aligned prior, the phase-structured correction reduces force-channel RMSE by $68.3\%$ for $f_{x,b}$, $15.0\%$ for $f_{y,b}$, and $69.8\%$ for $f_{z,b}$. The main axial and vertical force channels have high held-out $R^2$ values, $0.899$ and $0.909$, respectively. The lateral force channel remains the weakest force channel, with $R^2=0.276$ and $\mathrm{RMSE}/\sigma_y=0.851$.

These moment scores are reported as effective rotational-wrench prediction metrics under the measured inertia and smoothed angular-acceleration label definition, not as direct validation of isolated aerodynamic moments.

\subsection{Held-Out Log Variation}
\label{subsec:per_log_results}

The fixed test split contains four complete held-out logs from four flight dates. Table~\ref{tab:per_log_results} reports per-log RMSE for the final phase-structured correction. Force-channel RMSE varies from $1.444$ to $1.859~\mathrm{N}$ across logs, while moment-channel RMSE varies from $0.300$ to $0.425~\mathrm{N\,m}$. This variation is expected for outdoor data because each log carries a different combination of wind condition, maneuver content, estimator history, and wingbeat operating point. The current evidence therefore supports within-envelope held-out-log prediction, not broad extrapolation to unobserved maneuvers or other platforms.

\subsection{Replay Diagnostics and Claim Boundary}
\label{subsec:replay_claim_boundary}

The replay diagnostics clarify why the main validation remains log-conditioned prediction. The moment labels are algebraically consistent with the smoothed angular acceleration used to generate them: moment-inferred angular acceleration and smoothed angular acceleration differ by only about $2.6\times10^{-15}~\mathrm{rad/s^2}$ in norm RMSE. However, smoothed angular acceleration does not exactly integrate back to the raw logged gyro. With trapezoidal integration of the smoothed acceleration, the body-rate median error is $0.135~\mathrm{rad/s}$ at $0.10~\mathrm{s}$, $0.118~\mathrm{rad/s}$ at $0.25~\mathrm{s}$, $0.128~\mathrm{rad/s}$ at $0.50~\mathrm{s}$, and $0.139~\mathrm{rad/s}$ at $1.00~\mathrm{s}$.

This behavior is expected. Smoothing changes local slopes and suppresses spikes, which improves supervised label quality but changes the strict kinematic closure target. Outdoor replay also accumulates estimator noise, time-alignment error, wind disturbance, and controller-response mismatch. We therefore use replay as a diagnostic for label construction and do not present it as simulator-level validation.

\subsection{Interpretation and Limitations}
\label{subsec:interpretation_limitations}

The present results support a measured, log-conditioned correction of a low-dimensional flapping-wing simulator prior. The strongest evidence is in the axial and vertical force channels, together with the pitch-moment channel as a supporting effective-wrench output. These channels show stronger excitation, clearer phase or frequency structure, and lower normalized error. The weaker channels are not ignored; they identify where the current data, labels, and correction basis are less informative.

The evaluation is limited to one platform and a retained outdoor envelope. The labels are effective wrenches inferred from onboard state estimates, not direct wind-tunnel aerodynamic loads. The correction uses simulator-available inputs, but the present paper does not yet demonstrate closed-loop simulator deployment, reinforcement-learning training, or trajectory replay under modeled disturbances. Those steps remain future work after the instantaneous effective-wrench map and its uncertainty are better characterized.
